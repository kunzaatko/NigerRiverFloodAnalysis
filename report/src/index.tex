\label{sec:introduction}
This is an attempt at predicting the flood hazard of the \emph{Niger River}.

\subsection{Niger River}%
\label{sub:niger_river}
Niger is the main river of West Africa, with a length exceeding \qty{4100}{km}. Its drainage basin (area of land from
which all of the water ends up in the river) is over \qty{2.1e6}{km^2}. It originates in the Guinea Highlands and flows 
through Mali, Niger and Nigeria, before eventually reaching and discharging at the monumental Niger Delta. This 
trajectory makes it the 3\textsuperscript{rd} longest river in Africa only surpassed by the Congo River and, of course, 
the Nile.

Water flowing through the Niger river is significantly clearer compared to the Nile since it originates in ancient 
rocks providing little clay unlike the Nile. Similarly to Nile however, the Niger River floods annually usually in the 
time period from September to May, peaking in November.

\subsection{Data}%
\label{sub:data}
The data is downloaded from the publicly available \href{https://www.bafg.de/GRDC/EN/Home/homepage_node.html}{\ac{GRDC}}
database. The station, where the measurement took place is located in \emph{Niamey, Niger} (the capital city) at the river 
kilometre \qty{1420}{} (measured from the \emph{headwater} i.e. the furthest point upstream). The station's altitude is \qty{176}{m} above sea level and it covers a basin size of
\qty{791121}{km^2} (i.e. water flowing through this point of the river originates in this amount of area
upstream).

All measurements adhere to the standard notation in the branch, meaning that the discharges are denoted \(Q\). The data
consists of monthly mean measurements and daily measurements.

Monitoring at this station started in the year 1929, but in this analysis, we are only considering the 
span from 1944 to the present i.e. 2022 (disregarding the year 2023, because the data from this year is incomplete). 
We are not considering data from the start of the measurements due the fact that there are a lot of missing monthly records before this moment.

\subsection{Independence}%
\label{sub:independence}
For any kind of distribution-free estimation, we need to have the estimates of the mean \(\mu\). To approximate the
variance of the estimates, we first need to prove sequential independence of the time-series \(\left\{ R_{\max}(y) \right\}\) and 
\(\left\{ R_{\min}(y) \right\}\). As an alternative to testing independence, we will be testing sequential non-correlation.
That is, we are testing \[
    H_0: (\forall k \in \left[ h \right])(\rho_k(t) \coloneqq \Cov(X(t),\, X(t+k)) = 0),
\] 
where \(h\) is the maximum period of correlation that we allow and the hypothesis is taken for all \(t\) values that are
applicable. We are considering this hypothesis against the alternative of \[
    H_1: (\exists k \in \left[ h \right])(\rho_k(t) \neq 0).
\] 
The existence of such a \(k\) from hypothesis \(H_1\) would imply the time-series to have seasonality with the period of
\(k\) years.

We will be using the \emph{Ljung-Box test}. The statistic is \[
    Q = n(n+2) \sum_{k = 1}^{h} \frac{ \hat{\rho}_k^2 }{n - k}
\] and by \cite{ljungMeasureLackFit1978} under the assumption of \(H_0\), it holds asymptotically, that \(Q \sim \chi^2_{h}\).

\subsection{stationarity}%
\label{sub:stationarity}

One property that we will need for the estimates to be relevant however is strong stationarity. In other words, we need to
have confidence that the data comes from a single distribution, otherwise there is no reason for even estimating it. In
the terms of our data, we want to test that there is no apparent trend. That is, we would like to rule out the
possibility of water in the Niger river decreasing or increasing with time due to some external factor (e.g. climate
change). A great fit for this kind of statistical test would be \emph{Page's trend test} from \cite{pageContinuousInspectionSchemes1954}.

However I will only intuitively test weak stationarity by testing that even samples have the same mean as the odd 
samples using \emph{ANOVA} and similarly using the \emph{Levene test} for equal variances.

\section{Distribution-free Estimates}%
\label{sec:distribution-free_estimates}

We can make distribution-free estimates for objectives \Cref{obj:I,obj:II,obj:V}.

We will calculate an upper bound of the probability using Markov's inequality \[
    P(X > \varepsilon) \le \mathbb{E}X/\varepsilon \eqqcolon MUB,  
\] and Chebyšev's-Cantelli's inequality \[
    P(X - \mathbb{E}X > \varepsilon) \le \frac{\mathbb{D}X}{\mathbb{D}X + \varepsilon^2},
\]  therefore \[
     P(X > \varepsilon + \mathbb{E}X) \le \frac{\mathbb{D}X}{\mathbb{D}X + \varepsilon^2} \eqqcolon CCUB.
\] 
\begin{table}[H]
  \input{tables/distfree_estimates.tex}
  \caption{Distribution-free estimates for \Cref{obj:I,obj:II,obj:V}}
  \label{tab:dist_free}
\end{table}

We also have a distribution-free estimate for the lower bound in the \(L^{p}\) version of 
\emph{Paley-Zygmund inequality} \cite{petrovLowerBoundsTail2007} by
\[
    P(X > \theta\cdot \mathbb{E}X) \ge \frac{(1-\theta)^{p/(p-1)} \cdot \mathbb{E}[X]^{p/(p-1)}}{\mathbb{E}[X^p]^{1/(p-1)}} \eqqcolon PZLB_{p},
\] which holds for \(\theta \in \left< 0, 1 \right>\). This makes it a bad fit for the lower bounds of all of \(C\), \(D\)
and \(F\), since
\[
    \theta_{D} \ge \theta_{F} \ge \theta_{C} = C/\mathbb{E}[R_{\max}] \approx \frac{\qty{2031.5025}{\metre\cubed\per\second}}{\qty{1856.9178}{\metre\cubed\per\second}}
    \approx 1.095,
\] and this lower bound estimate is not good for \(\theta\) near \(0\) and \(1\) anyway.

But we can use this for
estimating the upper bound of the drought level in \Cref{obj:III}, since
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:PZLB_p}
    P(X \le E) = 1 - P(X > E) \le 1 - PZLB_{p}(E).
\end{equation}
Now from \Cref{eq:PZLB_p} we have an upper bound on the probability that is a function of \(p\), so we can also
maximize the \(PZLB_{p}\) in the \(p\) variable leading to the optimal upper bound constructed in this manner of \[
    P(R_{\max} \le E) \le 1 - PZLB_{12.7}(E) \approx 1 - 0.25104 = 0.74896,
\] and that is our distribution-free estimate for the upper bound of the drought probability, which unfortunately is
a very imprecise bound.

\section{Non-parametric Distribution Estimates}%
\label{sec:non-parametric_estimates}

We want to estimate the probability (not the bounds) for all of the objectives. For this we can also employ
non-parametric estimates. One such way of estimation is using a non-parametric density estimate and determining the bound
from this.

\subsection{Histogram Density Estimate}%
\label{sub:histogram_density_estimate}

The histogram estimate of the probability density of the random variable \(X\), for the (left closed) bins \[
    \begin{split}
        B_1 &= \left[\min(X)\eqqcolon m, m + \frac{M - m}{k}\right),\\
        B_2 &= \left[m + \frac{ M - m }{k}, m + 2\cdot\frac{M - m}{k}\right),\\
            &\vdots\\
        B_k &= \left[m + (k-1)\cdot\frac{M - m}{k}, M \coloneqq \max(X)\right]\\
    \end{split}
\]  is \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:histogram_density_estimate}
    \hat{p}_n^{H}(x) = \frac{k}{n}\sum_{i = 1}^{n} I(X_i \in B_\ell), \qq{such that} x \in B_\ell.
\end{equation}

We will use various methods for determining the optimal bin-size \(k\).
This means we are interested in selecting an optimal \(h\), where the number of bins is \[
    k = \left\lceil \frac{\max(R_{\max}) - \min(R_{\max})}{h} \right\rceil.
\] 

\subsubsection{Square-root choice}%
\label{ssub:square-root_choice}

Suggested bin-count \[
    k = \left\lceil \sqrt{n} \right\rceil.
\]
For our data this is \[
    k = \left\lceil \sqrt{79} \right\rceil = 9.
\] 

\subsubsection{Struge's Formula}%
\label{ssub:struge's_formula}

This method is derived from the binomial distribution and it suggests \[
    k = \left\lceil \log_2(n) \right\rceil + 1.
\]
For our data this is \[
    k = \left\lceil \log_2(79) \right\rceil + 1 = 7.
\] 

\subsubsection{Rice's Formula}%
\label{ssub:rice_formula}

Suggested bin-count \[
    k = \left\lceil 2 \sqrt[3]{n} \right\rceil.
\] 
For our data this is  \[
    k = \left\lceil 2 \sqrt[3]{79} \right\rceil = 9.
\] 


\subsubsection{Doane's Formula}%
\label{ssub:doane's_formula}

Suggested bin-count \[
    k = 1 + \left\lceil \log_2(n) + 
    \log_2 \left( 1 + \frac{\left| \widehat{\tilde{\mu}_3} \right|}{\sigma_{\widehat{\tilde{\mu}_3}}} \right)
\right\rceil,
\] where \(\widehat{\tilde{\mu}_3}\) is an estimator of skewness and \(\sigma_{\widehat{\tilde{\mu}_3}}\) is the
\ac{SD} of the estimate. For the estimator \[
    g_1 = \frac{m_3}{m_2^{3/2}}\qcomma m_2 = \widehat{\mathbb{E}[R_{\max}^2]} \qqtext{and} m_3
    = \widehat{\mathbb{E}[R_{\max}^3]},
\] the standard deviation is \[
    \sigma_{g_1} = \sqrt{\frac{6(n-2)}{(n+1)(n+3)}}.
\] 
For our data this suggests the bin-count of \[
    k = 9.
\]

\subsubsection{Freedman-Diaconi's Choice}%
\label{ssub:freedman-diaconi's_choice}

This rule suggests \[
    h = 2 \frac{\mathop{IQR}(x)}{\sqrt[3]{n}},
\] which for our data equates to \[
h \approx \qty{140.85}{\metre\cubed\per\second}.
\] and \[
    k = 11.
\]

\subsubsection{Histogram Estimates}%
\label{ssub:histogram_estimates}
We will select the histogram with \(11\) bins suggested by the choice \ref{ssub:freedman-diaconi's_choice}, because
the estimate of the \ac{IQR} is robust even if our data sample is not independent. We will be able to make
estimations for the threshold of \Cref{obj:I} and for the probability \(0.1\) of \Cref{obj:IV,obj:VI}.

Estimating the probability of the discharge passing the level \(T\) using a histogram estimate means calculating the
definite integral from \(T\) to \(+\infty\) of the histogram estimate of the probability density function at
\Cref{eq:histogram_density_estimate}. That is \[
    \hat{P}[X > T] = \int_{T}^{+\infty} \hat{p}_n^{\text{H}}(x) \dd{x}.
\] 

The reason why we need to be careful about this estimate and can not use it blindly for estimating any probability is
that if the threshold \(T\) is close to the extrema of the data, the estimated \(\hat{p}^H_n(x)\) in the integrand 
may be under or over estimated (has a large standard deviation) due to the lack of measurements in the integration
region. At the right edge of the histogram this is even more so
for distributions that are heavy-tailed. We also do not have an estimate of the probability density at values that are
greater than the maximum from the data or lower than the minimum of it and we cannot be sure, where the support of the
density really is. Moreover, using this definition of a histogram (one that is normalized to sum up to 1) does not 
allow us to estimate the probability of passing any threshold that is larger than the maximum of the data and that
limits us to the decade flood threshold (i.e. eliminating the ability to estimate for the probabilities of \(0.04\),
\(0.02\), \(0.01\) and \(0.004\) of objectives \Cref{obj:IV,obj:VI}).


That is the reason why we can only estimate these selected objectives from the histogram and not the rest, since they
are not represented in the data. For example \Cref{obj:III} cannot be estimated from the histogram we have here,
because, we do not know what the probability density estimate is under the minimum (it is equal to \(0\) using 
the histogram estimate) of the observed data and thus our estimate would not take this under consideration. We
will use the \ac{KDE} for estimating the other objectives. 


For \Cref{obj:I} we have the estimate of \[
    \hat{P}\left[R_{\max} \ge C\right] = \int_{C}^{+\infty} p_n^{H}(x) \dd{x} \approx 0.257.
\] 

Similarly for the threshold in \Cref{obj:IV} of the probability \(0.1\) we have \[
    \hat{P}^{-1}(0.1) = \qty{2181.665}{\metre\cubed\per\second} \eqqcolon T, 
\] where \[
    \hat{P}(T) = \hat{P}[ R_{\max} \ge T ].
\] 

For \Cref{obj:VI} we have to be careful about the question. It is asking about the conditional probability. The
estimate is as follows \[
    \begin{split}
        \hat{P}[ Z \le R_{\max} - C \mid R_{\max} \ge C ] &= \frac{\hat{P}[ Z + C \le R_{\max} ]}{\hat{P}[ R_{\max} \ge C ]}\\
                                                        &\approx \frac{0.045}{0.257} \approx 0.175.
    \end{split}
\]

\subsection{Kernel Density Estimate}%
\label{sub:kernel_density_estimate}

\ac{KDE} is a non-parametric method for estimating the probability density function of a random variable. It works by assigning a weight to each data point, and then summing the weighted kernels over the range of the variable. The kernel function is typically a bell-shaped curve, such as the Gaussian.

The following equation shows the \ac{KDE} of a random variable $X$ with data points $x_1, x_2, ..., x_n$:

\[
    \hat{p}_n^{\text{K}}(x) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n K\left( \frac{x - x_i}{\sigma} \right),
\]

where \(K\) is the kernel function, \(\sigma\) is the bandwidth parameter, and \(\hat{p}_n^{\text{K}}(x)\) is the estimated probability density function.

We will be choosing the bandwidth parameter and the kernel function visually for our data sample. The visual cues that
will determine our choice are the following:
\begin{description}
    \item[\label{itm:desc_cues_smoothness}Smoothness] \(\hat{p}_n^{\text{K}}\) should be smooth, not jagged and should not have sharp edges
    \item[\label{itm:desc_cues_consistency}Consistency] \(\hat{p}_n^{\text{K}}\) should be visually similar to the histogram estimate \(\hat{p}_n^{\text{H}}\)
\end{description}

The accuracy of the \ac{KDE} depends more no the choice of of \(\sigma\) than on the kernel \(K\) \cite{markovichClassicalMethodsProbability2007}
and therefore, we will be concentrating on the choice of the bandwidth parameter and use the, perhaps most widely used,
Epanechnikov kernel
\[
    K(x)=\frac{3}{4}(1-x^{2}).
\] 
Furthermore, we have extremal data, meaning that the distribution is presumably heavy-tailed. This creates a problem
of under-representation of the probability in the tail. There are three conventional methods for fighting this problem.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*)]
    \item combined parametric non-parametric methods
    \item variable bandwidth kernel estimator
    \item retransformed non-parametric estimator
\end{enumerate}

I will concentrate on the third option because of its simplicity and the possibility not to make any assumptions about
the distribution family. We will transform the data using the transformation

\begin{align}
    T(x) &= (2/\pi)\cdot \arctan(x) \\
    T^{-1}(y) &= \tan((\pi/2)\cdot y).
\end{align}

and the procedure is to transform the data creating a new \ac{RV} \(Y\), perform the non-parametric density
estimate and then use the inverse transform on the estimate produced. The point in this is that the support of the
transformed \ac{RV} is compact and therefore more suitable for \ac{KDE}
\cite{markovitchNonparametricEstimationLongtailed2000, markovichClassicalMethodsProbability2007}.

A good starting point for the bandwidth parameter \(\sigma\) is the \emph{Silverman's rule of thumb}
\cite{silvermanDensityEstimationStatistics1986}.
\[
    \sigma_{\text{Silverman's}} = 0.9 \cdot \min \left\{  \hat{\sigma}, \frac{\mathop{IQR}}{1.34} \right\} \cdot n^{-\frac{1}{5}},
\] 

where \(\mathop{IQR}\) is the interquartile range and \(\hat{\sigma}\) is the standard deviation estimate. This is
a rule choice that is optimal for a normally distributed sample. We will use is as a starting point for our choice.

The Silverman's rule of thumb gives 
\[
    \sigma_{\text{Silverman's}} \approx 1.538\times 10^{-5}
\]
for our transformed data \(T(X)\). We will make our choice visually considering the neighbourhood of the choice 
\(\sigma_{\text{Silverman's}}\) with the comparison with the histogram and taking into consideration
\hyperref[itm:desc_cues_consistency]{consistency} in the \Cref{fig:kde_choice_lines}.

\section{Parametric Estimates}%
\label{sec:parametric_estimates}

For a parametric estimate, we first need to find a parametric distribution that we expect the data comes from.

\subsection{Distribution Selection}%
\label{sub:distribution_selection}

We want to create \ac{MLE} fits of different distributions to the data while ensuring consistency with the previous
\ac{KDE} and histogram estimate of the density. From the previous estimates (e.g. \Cref{fig:kde_choice_plane}), it seems
apparent that the distribution is bimodal (explaining why the hypotheses that we simplified our requirement of
stationarity to in \Cref{sub:stationarity} didn't yield the desired result). As most of our hypothetical distributions
are unimodal, it is nonsensical to test these without filtering the data first (i.e. selecting data only from one of the
modes), but even with this precaution, the estimates would be appropriate only under the assumption that the modes
are isolated (i.e. that the distribution from the other mode does not affect the one that we would hypothetically be
fitting in isolation).

This models our physical expectation of the river discharge process well since, it would hypothetically arise from the
physical model of the river if there are two modes of the rivers discharge operation determined by external forces (e.g.
temperature, climate, etc.). This means that determined by these factors, the discharge in a particular year could be
modelled by a single one of the distributions from the mixture.

A mixture model complicates our hypothesised distribution however, because we are forced to consider mixtures of
different unimodal ones (extending the number of possibilities from \(n\) to \(n^2\)). After preliminary fits of
combinations of \emph{Gumbel}, \emph{Gamma} and \emph{Weibull} distribution mixtures, I selected two parametric
distributions.\footnote{The likelihood equations system is not guaranteed to be convex for mixture
distributions in particular, so it is necessary to find initial parameters \(p_0\) for the distribution that are
close enough to the optimal solution to ensure that the \ac{MLE} is the global solution.}
Namely a mixture containing two Gumbel distributions and one that explains the first mode with a Gamma distribution and
the second with a Gumbel. The two Q-Q plots of the parametric fits can be seen in \Cref{fig:QQ_selection_mixture} and we
can compare them with the previous \ac{KDE} density function in \Cref{fig:PD_selection_mixture}.

\section{The Number of Record Values}%
\label{sec:the_number_of_record_values}%

\Cref{obj:V} also requires an estimate for the number of record values that will occur in the next 50 years. For
a sequence of \(n\) \ac{iid} random variables \((X_i)_{i=1}^n\), we define a maximal record indicator as \[
    Y_i = \begin{cases}
        1 & \text{if } i = 1 \text{ or } i > 1 \text{ and } X_i > \max_{j < i} \{ X_j\}\\
        0 & \text{otherwise.}
    \end{cases}
\] 

With this notation, what we consider the number of records in \(n\) time periods is \[
    M_n = \sum_{i = 1}^{n} Y_i.
\] 

What allows us to determine the probability \(P[Y_i = 1]\) is the realization that \[
    X_i > \max_{j < i} \{ X_j \} \Leftrightarrow F(X_i) > \max_{j < i} \{ F(X_j)\}),
\] 
where \(F(X)\) is the cumulative distribution function of \(X\). We know that \(F(X)\) has a uniform distribution
\(U_{[0,1]}\) and thus for the maximal record value indicator (an analogous derivation can be done for the minimum record
value indicator with the same result) it holds that \[
    P[Y_i = 1] = 1/i.
\] 

Thus the expected number of records in \(n\) time periods~is \[
    \mathbb{E}[M_n] = \mathbb{E}\left[ \sum_{i = 1}^{n} Y_i \right] = \sum_{i = 1}^{n} 1/i.
\] 

This however further argues in the direction of our sequence not being \ac{iid}, because the expected number of records
in our sequence of measurements would be \(\sum_{i = 1}^{79} 1/i \approx 5\) but there are \(13\) record values in our
sample. There is a test for assessing whether a sample is \ac{iid} based on this result, that is derived in
\cite{benestadRecordvaluesNonstationarityTests2004} that is based on determining the confidence interval using Monte
Carlo simulations. Using a similar simulation the \ac{SD} of \(M_n\) with \(n \equiv 79\) is \(1.8217\). The reliability
of the sample \(M_n\) being different from the theoretical and therefore the sample not being \ac{iid} is
\(4.42\sigma\).

However if we considered an \ac{iid} sample, then the number of expected record measurements in the next \(50\) years
(i.e. from 2023 to 2073) is \[
    M_{[80,129]} \coloneqq \mathbb{E}\left[ \sum_{i = 80}^{129} Y_i \right] = \sum_{i = 80}^{129} 1/i \approx 0.49.
\] 
A Monte Carlo simulation of the \ac{SD} of \(M_{[80,129]}\) is \(0.69\).


\section{Summary and Discussion}%
\label{sec:summary}

The different approaches to estimating the distributions in
\Cref{sub:histogram_density_estimate}, \ref{sub:kernel_density_estimate} and \ref{sec:parametric_estimates}
are mutually consistent as can be seen in
\Cref{fig:gumbel_mixture_fits}. This is an assurance of their respective correctness (although they are based one on the
other which slightly undercuts this). The different estimates are also consistent with the distribution-free lower bounds
developed in \Cref{sec:distribution-free_estimates}. This means that selection of the distribution estimate does not
change the objective estimates by a large amount and we can select any one of them.

As stated above, the distribution of \(R_\text{max}\) does not seem to be stationary and this should be further
investigated. Another hint for the non-stationarity can be observed in \Cref{fig:seasonality_of_discharges}, where it
seems the measurements from the beginnings of the years are growing with the pass of time in the mean.

Based on the Q-Q plot in \Cref{fig:gumbel_mixture_fits}, the Gumbel mixture is a good fit. Therefore I select the 
parametric model estimates. This is further
supported by the fact that the Gumbel distribution is often used in flood forecasting models. It is an interesting
conclusion that the river seems to have two modes of functioning. The final estimates are summarized in the table
\Cref{tab:summary_estimates}.

For a regulator that is planning on making an anti-flood barrier on the river near the Niamey facility the recommended
height is dependent on the planned lifetime of this barrier as well as the other possibilities to avoid a disastrous
level of water. Also the price of a meter of the barrier is presumably not constant as a variable of its height. This
should be taken into consideration. I would suggest the 100-year water level (\(\qty{2547.7}{\meter\cubed\per\second}\))
and the barrier height should be derived from this value, but only after further investigation into the stationarity.
